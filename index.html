Do not submit pure, unedited AI output. Mission & values

Mission & Values

Cioré is exploring a more thoughtful direction for personal technology.

We're researching how a future phone experience could place more emphasis on user control, privacy, repairability, accessibility, responsible sourcing, and long-term usefulness. We're still early in the process, so we're honest about what is being researched, what is being planned, and what needs more evidence before it can become a commitment.

We're building this direction with contributors, communities, researchers, designers, and people who believe technology should serve people with more care.

Purpose

Our Mission

Cioré exists to explore and work toward technology that respects people, supports user control, and considers the lasting impact of the choices behind it.

We're interested in challenging assumptions about how phones are designed, repaired, powered, updated, communicated through, and understood by the people who use them.

This work is still developing. Many directions in the Contributor Hub are research areas, early concepts, or planning notes. They aren't final specifications, product promises, or confirmed outcomes.

Our values

What Guides Us

Truth

We're honest about what we know, what we don't know, and what still needs research, testing, evidence, or review.

We don't overstate progress. We don't present concepts, prototypes, or future exploration as finished products. When something is uncertain, we say so.

Transparency

We communicate clearly and explain our thinking when we can.

We document sources, assumptions, questions, tradeoffs, and changes in direction. We want contributors and future users to understand not only what we're exploring, but why.

Community Service

We stay connected to the people and communities technology affects.

We listen to lived experience, feedback, and concerns. We share knowledge responsibly, give back where we can, and remember that technology should improve people's lives instead of adding to their burden.

Legacy

We're working toward something that matters beyond the present moment.

We think about the long-term impact of technology on people, communities, workers, repairability, resources, privacy, and the environment. We want to build carefully enough that our choices can stand up over time.

Care and Safety

We're committed to creating a respectful, welcoming, and safer space for contributors, future users, collaborators, and communities.

Care shows up in how we communicate, how we respect boundaries, how we protect internal information, how we consider accessibility, and how we respond when something needs attention.

In practice

How These Values Show Up in Our Work

In research

We look for credible sources, state uncertainty, compare tradeoffs, and keep learning when evidence changes.

In communication

We use clear language, avoid unsupported claims, and make space for questions, feedback, and different perspectives.

In community

We respect people's names, pronouns, time zones, communication preferences, needs, and lived experience.

In product direction

We're researching approaches related to modularity, repairability, privacy, accessibility, responsible sourcing, energy and thermal questions, communication resilience, and user control. Every direction still requires its own research, technical validation, supplier evidence, security review, accessibility review, legal review, or hardware testing.

In decision-making

We don't treat a good idea as a final decision until it has the evidence, context, review, and care it needs.

Where we are

Our Responsibility as an Early-Stage Team

Being early-stage gives us room to learn, but it also gives us responsibility.

We're responsible for being clear when something is active work, research, a prototype concept, future exploration, or not a product claim. We're responsible for listening when people raise concerns. We're responsible for changing course when evidence, safety, accessibility, or community feedback shows that we should.

We won't always have every answer yet. We can still choose to be honest, thoughtful, and accountable while we work toward better ones.

Join us

A Space for Bold and Creative Minds

Cioré needs bold thinkers, careful researchers, creative problem-solvers, organizers, designers, writers, engineers, accessibility advocates, and people who care enough to ask better questions.

Your perspective matters here. You don't need to have every answer. You do need to bring curiosity, care, honesty, and a willingness to learn alongside others.

Code of Conduct

This Code of Conduct explains how we work together at Cioré.

By participating in Cioré spaces or workstreams, you agree to follow these standards. They apply in the Contributor Hub, Discord, project workspaces, meetings, email, calls, documents, and any public or private communication connected to Cioré.

1.Why This Exists

We're building Cioré around people, care, and honest work.

This Code helps create a space where contributors can ask questions, share ideas, collaborate respectfully, protect internal information, and raise concerns without fear of being dismissed.

We don't expect perfection. We do expect honesty, care, accountability, and a willingness to learn.

This Code is grounded in our shared values — Truth, Transparency, Community Service, Legacy, and Care and Safety. Read more on Mission & Values.

2.How We Work Together

Treat people with respect.

Be kind, professional, and fair in Discord, meetings, documents, email, and every other Cioré space.

Use people's correct names and pronouns. Respect time zones, availability, DM preferences, communication styles, and personal boundaries.

Communicate early.

If you're blocked, need more time, need context, or your availability changes, let the relevant project contact or channel know as early as you can. You don't need to have everything figured out before asking for help.

Give and receive feedback with care.

Be open to thoughtful feedback. Share concerns respectfully and focus on the work, not personal attacks. Assume good intent when reasonable, but don't ignore conduct that feels harmful, unsafe, or inappropriate.

Give credit.

Acknowledge other people's ideas, research, writing, designs, and contributions. Don't take credit for work that isn't yours.

Keep communication organized.

Use the appropriate Discord channel or project workspace whenever possible. If you're not sure where something belongs, ask. Keeping work in the right place helps everyone stay informed.

3.Independent Work and Project Guidance

Most contributor work at Cioré is self-directed.

You don't need to wait for someone to personally assign you a task before exploring an active work area. Review What We Are Working On Now, read the available project context, and choose a task or contribution that fits your skills, interests, and capacity.

When a project has a project manager or designated contact, coordinate with them before beginning. Follow the project's guidance, priorities, and communication process.

When no project contact is listed:

  • Review the available research, task guidance, and project workspace before starting.
  • Keep your notes, sources, drafts, files, and observations organized.
  • Share progress, questions, and relevant context in the appropriate project channel.
  • Ask before making assumptions that could affect safety, security, privacy, accessibility, supplier decisions, legal or regulatory questions, public statements, or product direction.

Independent work doesn't mean working alone. Ask for support when you need it.

4.Research Integrity and Responsible Tool Use

Cioré values careful research and honest communication.

When you share research, recommendations, comparisons, or findings:

  • Identify your sources whenever possible.
  • Separate facts, opinions, assumptions, and open questions.
  • Explain uncertainty or missing evidence.
  • Avoid copying information without checking where it came from.
  • Don't present incomplete research as final company direction.

AI and other tools may help with brainstorming, organization, drafting, or analysis. You're still responsible for the work you submit.

Before sharing AI-assisted work:

  • Check facts, sources, names, dates, claims, and technical details.
  • Use your own judgment and add your own thinking.
  • Don't submit unedited AI output as verified research or original contributor work.
  • Don't upload confidential Cioré information into unapproved tools.
  • Ask if you're unsure whether a tool is appropriate for internal material.

5.Research-Stage Language and Public Claims

Cioré is early-stage. Many topics in the Contributor Hub are research directions, planning notes, prototype concepts, or future explorations.

Don't describe these topics as finished products, confirmed features, final specifications, or public commitments.

Don't make unsupported claims about:

  • Product availability, pricing, preorders, shipping, or launch dates.
  • Privacy, security, encryption, safety, accessibility, sustainability, repairability, or performance.
  • App compatibility, carrier support, emergency communications, range, certification, legal compliance, or regional availability.
  • Suppliers, materials, labor practices, sourcing, battery chemistry, or environmental impact.
  • Hardware features, operating-system capabilities, AI behavior, or technical outcomes.

Use accurate language such as:

  • “Cioré is researching...”
  • “Cioré is exploring...”
  • “Cioré is working toward...”
  • “This is an early concept...”
  • “This needs technical validation...”
  • “This is not yet a product promise...”

Don't speak publicly on behalf of Cioré unless the founder has approved the message.

6.Confidentiality and Access

What happens inside Cioré stays inside Cioré unless you have clear permission to share it.

Keep internal material private, including:

  • Contributor information and directory details.
  • Access codes, login details, Discord invites, and internal links.
  • Research notes, planning documents, meeting notes, recordings, screenshots, and files.
  • Prototype observations, design work, product concepts, supplier information, technical discussions, and internal decisions.
  • Messages, conversations, or documents shared through the Contributor Hub, Discord, email, meetings, or project workspaces.

Don't share internal material outside approved Cioré spaces without explicit founder approval.

If you're unsure whether something can be shared, ask first.

Protect your access. Don't share your personal access code or invite links. Log out when using a shared device. Report suspicious access, accidental sharing, or a possible security issue to the founder as soon as possible.

7.Data, Tools, and Workspaces

Use approved Cioré tools and workspaces for contributor communication, file storage, research, and collaboration.

Don't move Cioré material into personal accounts, unapproved apps, public tools, or external platforms without permission.

If your work involves contributor, waitlist, survey, or other personal information:

  • Handle it carefully.
  • Only use it for approved Cioré work.
  • Don't download, share, sell, reuse, or publish it.
  • Ask before exporting, combining, or moving it into another tool.

Phone direction

The Phone

Cioré is exploring a phone that feels different from the phones most people are used to. We want it to feel smooth in our users’ hands, easier to understand, easier to repair, and more personal over time.

We’re still researching and building this direction. Some parts are early ideas. Some parts are being compared. Some may change as we learn more. This page gives our team the current shared direction so we can create, research, question, and contribute with the same starting point.

A quick note before you begin

This page is a guide for contributors, not a final product announcement. Please treat everything here as current direction and research context. If you notice something missing, unclear, outdated, or worth exploring, tell the founder on Discord.

Concept evolution

Concept Evolution

These visuals show the direction we are exploring. They are design studies, not final product renders, final materials, or final hardware specifications.

The feeling we are designing for

A phone that feels considered

Cioré is exploring a phone shaped around comfort, clarity, and long term care.

We want the phone to feel balanced in our users’ hands. We want to give people more choice around repair, charging, privacy, accessibility, and the physical parts they use every day.

The phone should feel like something a person keeps, cares for, and makes their own. It should not feel disposable.

Comfort

A body shape that aims to reduce harsh edges, pressure points, and awkward grip.

Control

Clear settings, thoughtful physical interaction, and options that do not rely on only one type of input.

Repair

Parts that can be researched for replacement, repair, or future updates instead of treating the whole phone as disposable.

Choice

More room for people to decide how they charge, store, use, and care for their device.

Physical direction

Why the phone is rounded

Cioré is exploring a continuous pill shaped body with fully rounded ends.

The goal is to learn whether this shape can feel balanced, smooth, and comfortable in our users’ hands during everyday use.

The rounded form is also part of the phone’s visual identity. It is meant to feel grounded, easy to recognize, and considered from every angle.

Materials and finish

A darker, more thoughtful surface

Cioré is exploring a dark smoked back surface that looks simple at first glance but can reveal depth, materials, or soft light in certain conditions.

We are also exploring recycled tempered glass for the front and back of the phone. The goal is to look at materials that can feel premium while supporting longer life, repair, and more thoughtful sourcing.

Nanocellulose is another material we want to research. It is a plant based material that may be useful as a thin reinforcement layer. We need to learn more about durability, heat, appearance, repairability, and how it could work with other materials before making decisions.

We are not making final claims yet about drop protection, scratch resistance, water resistance, recycled content, material safety, or long term durability.

The screen

A screen that is clear and easier to use

The display needs to look good, but it also needs to be easy to read, easy to adjust, and comfortable for different people.

We are exploring a high quality OLED display direction with deep blacks, strong readability, and a softer visual experience. We are also interested in ways to create a more transparent or layered look without making the screen harder to see or use.

What we want to research

  • Text size and spacing
  • High contrast options
  • Brightness controls
  • Color and motion settings
  • Dyslexia friendly reading choices
  • Low vision support
  • Screen reader support
  • Haptic, audio, and visual feedback
  • Voice access
  • Settings that are easy to find and understand
  • Ways to reduce visual overload

A beautiful screen is not enough. If someone cannot read it, reach it, understand it, or use it comfortably, we need to keep working.

Controls and interaction

More than one way to use the phone

Cioré is exploring several ways people may interact with the phone, including touch, tactile controls, physical button options, audio feedback, haptic feedback, voice access, and on screen alternatives.

Touch and sensor directions

  • Side tactile zones along the edges for swiping up or down, tapping, or holding
  • Volume controls that respond to a swipe up or swipe down motion
  • A top sensor area for single tap, double tap, or hold shortcuts such as camera, flashlight, or voice notes
  • Assignable actions such as camera shortcuts, focus modes, media controls, or navigation
  • A right side sensor direction for power control using a deliberate hold action
  • Settings that let people choose whether side and top areas respond to taps, swipes, or holds

Accessibility and physical button choices

  • Adjustable touch sensitivity, hold to confirm requirements, and options to turn off swipe gestures
  • Physical dedicated button options and modular button attachments for people who prefer mechanical click feedback
  • Grip attachments and case designs with larger physical controls
  • Audio, visual, and haptic feedback options for every action
  • On screen alternatives for every physical gesture

These controls are still being explored. We need to learn what feels natural, what prevents accidental touches, what works for left handed and right handed use, and what physical alternatives are needed for people with tremor, chronic pain, or limited dexterity.

We want to hear from people who use assistive technology, have tremor, chronic pain, limited grip strength, low vision, blindness, hearing differences, dyslexia, motion sensitivity, fatigue, or different hand sizes. Your feedback can change the direction of this work.

Light, warmth, and heat

Exploring extra energy without ignoring safety

We are researching small ways the phone may collect extra energy from sunlight, bright ambient light, and temperature differences around the device.

The light based part is the Energy Backplate. The warmth based part would use small test strips designed to collect a tiny amount of energy when one part is warmer and another part is cooler.

Touch by itself does not charge a phone. A warmth based strip needs a temperature difference. For example, a warm hand on one side and cooler air on another side may create a very small amount of energy.

What warmth based strips may be useful for

  • Learning whether heat differences can support very low power features
  • Exploring a tiny energy reserve
  • Testing how light, heat, and stored power could work together
  • Helping us understand what is realistic before making promises

What they are not expected to do

  • Fully recharge the phone
  • Replace USB-C
  • Quickly raise the battery percentage
  • Create meaningful charging energy from ordinary touch alone

Heat needs to be part of the design from the start

Sunlight can warm the back of a phone. Batteries also need safe temperatures. We need to research how to collect energy without making the phone too hot, uncomfortable to hold, or unsafe to charge.

  • How hot the Energy Backplate gets in direct sunlight
  • How to keep heat away from the battery
  • How to spread heat instead of trapping it
  • When energy collection should slow down or pause
  • How cases, grips, and accessories affect heat
  • How the phone should clearly tell someone when it is too warm

If the phone gets too warm, it should protect itself, pause or slow energy collection when needed, and explain what is happening in clear language.

Cioré is exploring a phone that people can keep for longer, care for, repair, adapt, and potentially improve over time instead of replacing the entire device whenever one part wears out or their needs change.

Product life

A Different Path for Product Life

Most modern smartphones are built as permanently sealed objects. While dense adhesive seals can help devices stay thin and water resistant, they can also make basic repairs difficult, expensive, or impractical for ordinary users.

Our long-term goal is not to encourage people to keep buying new phones. We want to make it more realistic for people to keep one Cioré phone longer.

Standard model

Minor damage or a worn part can lead to replacing the entire phone.

Cioré direction

A worn part, changing need, or comfort issue could lead to servicing a part or adapting the system.

This requires thinking about the device as a system that can be opened, maintained, and updated where safe and practical, supported by clear documentation and accessible design choices.

Inclusive design

Designing for Accessible Repair

A phone can be technically repairable with tools, yet still remain inaccessible to the person holding it. Repairability also depends on the physical and cognitive friction of the repair process itself.

Technical repairability

Parts can be separated with tools.

Accessible repair

The repair process is clear, safe, and manageable for diverse human needs.

Barriers we are studying

Fine fasteners

Small screws can be difficult to see, hold, or align for people with tremor or low vision.

High opening force

Casings or clips can require high hand strength, pinch force, or prying leverage.

Delicate ribbon cables

Thin flex cables and small connectors can tear easily during handling.

Visual ambiguity

Internal components can be hard to identify if they lack clear orientation markings.

Complex instructions

Dense repair manuals can create unnecessary cognitive overload.

Potential solutions under review

Clear fastener choices

Evaluating larger, standardized screw drives that may resist stripping and reduce driver slipping.

Guided cable paths

Researching reinforced connectors and orientation marks that are color-coded but not dependent on color alone.

Step-by-step visual guides

Testing plain-language repair instructions with high-contrast diagrams, audio options, and video guidance.

Service boundaries

Clearly identifying tasks that may be suitable for user repair and tasks that may require trained technician support.

Engineering reality

Balancing the Tradeoffs

Modularity is not a free choice. Every removable panel, extra screw, connector, and external attachment introduces physical, electrical, and manufacturing tradeoffs.

Objective Engineering tradeoff What we are researching
Screw assembly May increase device thickness and frame weight compared with permanent adhesive assembly. Structural frame materials and internal layout efficiency.
Water protection Removable panels need gaskets and seals that can degrade or misalign. Compression gaskets, O-rings, sealing strategy, and realistic ingress-protection boundaries.
Replaceable battery A protective enclosure and safe connector system may reduce available space or energy density. Battery pouch enclosures versus hard-shell battery-pack directions.

Spare-parts life Supporting replacement parts over years creates inventory and logistics complexity. Part standardization, compatibility planning, and sustainable support models.

Sandboxing and sharing

How Lumé Handles App Access

Lumé builds on Android app sandboxing and permission controls. Sandboxing means apps are placed in separate spaces so they cannot freely read each other's private data. Apps can still share information when a user intentionally asks them to do something useful, such as sending a photo, opening a map location, using a payment service, signing in, sharing a file, using a password manager, or using an accessibility tool.

Lumé's concern includes unnecessary data sharing connected to profiling, advertising, behavioral targeting, cross app tracking, spending habit targeting, and background collection. Lumé can use Android permissions, profiles, system sharing mechanisms, user controlled restrictions, and selected activity indicators where technically supportable. Lumé must not claim it can see, stop, or control every action inside every app.

How We Want the Phone to Charge

Cioré is researching a more thoughtful approach to power, one that stays dependable for everyday use while exploring supplemental energy, better battery care, and clearer information over time.

This research lives under Nyro, Cioré's developing energy and thermal direction. Nothing here is a confirmed feature or final specification.

Nyro system

Nyro: Cioré's Energy and Thermal Direction

Nyro is Cioré's developing energy and thermal system direction. It brings together how a phone receives power, stores it, protects its battery, manages heat, explains energy use, and may use carefully tested supplemental energy inputs over time.

Nyro connects the whole power experience: how users get power, how the phone receives and stores it, how the battery is protected, how heat is managed, how power use is explained, how a battery may be serviced, how supplemental energy research could be measured and controlled, and how charging components might be returned, repaired, reused, or recovered over time.

Charging priorities

USB-C First, Wireless Charging Under Research

USB-C remains the baseline that any Nyro research direction has to work alongside, not complicate. It is the charging path we expect people to rely on every day.

Wireless charging is a separate research area. We are comparing dock, click-in, guided-position, and coil-based alignment concepts, and weighing each one against accessibility, heat, durability, and repair access before any direction is chosen.

Connection and preparedness

Offline Capability

Cioré is working toward a phone that stays helpful even when the grid or cell network is unavailable. The goal is to build a phone that uses the best connection around, then gracefully shifts to offline tools, local radio, and nearby sharing when normal networks drop.

This page outlines how we are approaching offline capability, local radio, nearby connections, and the charging base concept so contributors can find where to research and contribute.

Use What Is Available

Use What Is Available

The phone is being designed to choose the most dependable path available without forcing someone to configure complex settings.

Cellular service available
Use the cellular network ↓ Cellular service limited, Wi-Fi available
Use supported Wi-Fi calling ↓ No cell service, no Wi-Fi
Use offline maps, saved information, local radio, and nearby connection research

When full connectivity is present, everything works as expected. When service drops, the phone steps down to local connections and stored tools rather than leaving someone with an empty screen.

How Cellular Service Works

How Cellular Service Works

Most modern phones rely constantly on cell towers and central servers to show maps, load messages, and verify data. Cell towers connect a phone to a provider network, while Wi-Fi routers bridge a device to broadband internet.

When a storm, power outage, or remote location cuts off those networks, standard apps often stop working because they depend on remote servers. Offline capability means designing software and hardware so essential features run directly on the phone itself.

What Stays Useful Offline

What Stays Useful Offline

When no network is available, the phone should still serve as a reliable tool. We are exploring ways to keep these core features working offline.

Offline maps and navigation for local areas
Saved reading, notes, documents, contacts, and personal guides
Stored emergency checklists and local resource guides
Clear connection status indicators so someone knows when they are truly offline
Battery aware settings that reduce background power draw while using offline tools

Local Radio

FM reception may be integrated directly into the phone. We are testing whether wired headphones in the 3.5 mm jack can serve as the antenna, alongside research into a small internal antenna.

When connected to the base, the phone provides the screen, tuning controls, speakers, and accessibility settings for local broadcasts.

Materials, sourcing and sustainability

Materials, Sourcing & Sustainability

Cioré is working toward a phone that considers its materials, parts, suppliers, repair options, and end of life from the beginning.

We want to keep looking for healthier and lower impact alternatives without pretending every choice has a perfect answer. This page explains what we look for, how we compare options, and how contributors can help us make better informed decisions.

Why this work matters

A phone carries a long story

A phone may look small, but it depends on materials, components, energy, manufacturing, packaging, shipping, software support, repair options, and end of life decisions.

Harm can happen at different points in that process. That is why Cioré looks beyond the finished phone and asks questions about the full path behind every part.

Part of the phone’s life What Cioré wants to explore

Manufacturing Suppliers that share useful information about facilities, safety, labor, and materials
Chemicals and finishes Clearer information about coatings, adhesives, inks, solvents, and material safety

End of life Repair first, then reuse, parts recovery, and responsible recycling research

Our commitment

We want to know what stands behind the phone

Cioré is working toward a phone built with more visibility, care, and responsibility at every stage.

We will keep looking for better options and evidence, and we are working toward closer long term responsibility for assembly, distribution, repair support, and parts recovery.

How we decide

How Cioré makes decisions

Start with what the part needs to do ↓ Look for lower impact and repairable options ↓ Check supplier evidence ↓ Compare safety, durability, cost, access, and repair tradeoffs ↓ Document what is known and what still needs review ↓ Choose carefully or keep researching

A lower impact option still has to work safely in a real phone. We want to make the most responsible workable choice based on evidence.

Our checks

Five questions we ask

Does it work?

The material or part needs to do its job reliably and safely.

Can it be repaired?

We look for parts that can be accessed, replaced, or maintained where practical.

Can we trace it?

We want useful information about material origin, manufacturing, and supplier practices.

Does it reduce harm?

We compare waste, extraction, chemicals, energy, packaging, and end of life impact.

Does it serve people?

The choice should consider comfort, accessibility, cost, reliability, and the people making the product.

Research notes

How to write research notes

Record what you found, what you could not find, and what needs follow up.

Useful research note

Factory location is listed as Monterrey, Mexico. The supplier shows an ISO 14001 certificate and links to a current document. A third party labor audit is mentioned. No specific wage information or raw material origin was found.

Still useful research note

The supplier says sustainability is important but does not describe specific practices. No certifications were found. Manufacturing location is not listed. Follow up questions are needed before this supplier can be rated higher.

Cioré is designing toward a phone that is easier to hold, see, hear, understand, control, charge, repair, and keep using over time.

Starting with people

What Accessibility Means Here

Accessibility means designing a phone so more people can use it, understand it, and keep using it with less unnecessary effort.

Accessibility includes disability access, but it also includes aging, temporary injury or recovery, chronic pain, fatigue, different hand sizes, speech differences, neurodivergence, caregiving, bright sunlight, noise, stress, and changing needs over time.

Accessibility is not

One setting, one mode, or one imagined default user.

Accessibility is

A process of giving people clearer choices and reducing unnecessary friction across the phone, software, accessories, repair, charging, and support.

We're not designing one phone for one imagined user. We're researching how Cioré can give more people useful choices, clear information, and less unnecessary friction in everyday life.

Accessibility is not one setting, one mode, or one person's definition of ease. We're designing toward more choices so people can shape the phone around their needs without needing to become an expert first.

Research with care

How We Keep Accessibility Grounded

Accessibility work is not complete when a feature is listed in a plan. It needs research, prototyping, testing, feedback, revision, and honest communication about what works and what still needs improvement.

1

Collect feedback from people with lived experience, caregivers, families, contributors, and communities.

2

Understand

Turn feedback into clear themes, research questions, constraints, and design goals.

3

Explore

Compare possible hardware, software, documentation, accessory, charging, and support approaches.

4

Test

Test concepts with people who have different access needs and use different tools, settings, languages, and ways of interacting.

5

Improve

Document findings, acknowledge tradeoffs, revise the work, and keep learning.

Automated checks and design guidelines can help, but they do not replace direct feedback from people who use phones differently from the team building them.

We won't assume one approach works for everyone. Some needs may require different solutions, some features may take longer to validate, and some tradeoffs need careful testing.

A good first contribution

What a Helpful Contribution Looks Like

You do not need to solve an entire workstream to make a useful contribution. A clear, well-scoped first step helps the team learn, decide, and build momentum.

Type of contribution Good first contribution What to include
Research A short comparison or landscape note focused on one clear question. Sources, dates, relevant facts, what the information means for Cioré, gaps, risks, assumptions, and open questions.
Design A labeled early concept, wireframe, visual reference, template, or workflow draft tied to an existing question. Purpose, intended user or contributor need, known constraints, what is assumed, and what feedback is needed.
Writing A plain-language rewrite, project brief, FAQ draft, outreach template, onboarding guide, or documentation improvement. Who it is for, what it helps them understand or do, what information it relies on, and what still needs review.
Planning and coordination A project outline, research tracker, milestone draft, workflow map, meeting agenda, decision log, or organized source library. Scope, owner or contact, dependencies, decisions needed, next steps, and what is not yet decided.
Engineering or technical research A feasibility note, risk list, test plan, architecture question, prototype observation, or technical research summary. Problem being studied, current evidence, assumptions, risks, technical unknowns, required expertise, and recommended next question.
Community research An anonymized synthesis of feedback themes or a structured list of user needs. No names, contact details, quotes that identify someone, personal data, or unapproved external sharing.

Project status

How to Read Project Status

Open for contributor support

Context is available and contributors can begin with a small, well-scoped step after posting in the project channel.

Needs project owner

The direction is important, but it needs someone to help organize scope, questions, documentation, or coordination before broader work begins.

Researching

The team is gathering evidence, comparing options, or clarifying the problem before moving into implementation.

In review

Work has been submitted or discussed and is waiting for Founder or project-lead review.

Workspace forming

Hub context exists, but the detailed Notion project workspace is still being organized.

Paused

The project is not accepting new work right now. Review context only unless the Founder reopens it.

Later

This direction matters, but it should not take attention away from current priorities.

An interesting research page is not always an active project. Check the status and project channel before beginning substantial work.
